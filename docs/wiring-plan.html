5×5×5 Addressable Cube Wiring

A build plan for a 125-LED RGB cube on 4-leg addressable LEDs, driven by an Arduino Nano. No driver ICs, no layer transistors, three wires from the cube to the board. This revision is drawn for the LEDs actually bought and the legs actually measured, which changed the structure: the cube is held up by its own wire framework, not by the LED legs.

What changed from revision 1

Revision 1 assumed APA106 parts with legs long enough to reach from one layer to the next. Neither held.

  • Pinout. The LEDs are WS2812-family, pin order DIN · GND · VDD · DOUT. That is the middle two pins swapped from the APA106 order the first revision was drawn for. Getting this wrong destroys the LED instantly.
  • Leg lengths. GND 16.5 mm, VDD 15 mm, DIN and DOUT 14 mm each — too short to span the 20.32 mm layer pitch. The vertical columns are now pillars of 0.9 mm tinned copper wire that each LED's VDD leg solders onto, rather than legs soldered to legs.
  • Pitch. 20.32 mm, which is 8 holes of 0.1″ stripboard, matching the existing 4×4×4 and letting the cube stand on a stripboard base.
  • Layers are no longer rotated. All five are now built identically on one jig, at the cost of one routed data jumper per layer boundary. Rotating them is incompatible with pillars that must line up vertically.
  • Sketch settings. WS2812B and GRB, not APA106 and RGB.

The LED

Four legs in a row. The two outer ones carry data through the chain, the two inner ones are the supply. Everything in this plan follows from which leg is which, so confirm it on your own parts before bending 125 of them — put 100 Ω in series with the 5 V feed and the wrong way round costs you nothing.

Why VDD on the pillars and GND on the rails

Both conductors carry identical current, so the choice is not about capacity. Three things decide it, and all three point the same way.

ReasonConsequence
The data return pathData hops sideways between neighbours within a row. With GND running as a rail alongside each row, the signal and its return are a close pair. Put GND on the verticals instead and every 20 mm data hop returns down a pillar, across the base and back up — a loop a hundred times larger.
Leg lengthsGND is the longest leg at 16.5 mm, so it is the one to spend on a horizontal reach. A vertical leg only has to touch a pillar that runs past it, which takes a few millimetres whatever its length.
Where the heavy copper goes25 pillars land on the base board, where VDD can be distributed through reinforced stripboard and thick wire. GND gets back to the base through four corner risers, which is ample for the current involved.

One layer

All five layers are identical, built on the same jig. Data snakes along the rows, so alternate rows are turned 180° — that is what lets each DOUT reach the DIN beside it with the LEDs' own legs. Turning a row does not disturb the supply legs, because they leave at right angles to the pin row: GND still reaches the rail behind, VDD still reaches the pillar in front.

The stack

The pillars are what make this a cube rather than five loose layers. Each is one continuous piece of 0.9 mm tinned copper wire running from the base board to the top layer, with five VDD legs soldered to it along the way. Build the layers flat, then thread them down the pillars like beads.

The base board and where power goes in

Use a 100 × 160 mm Eurocard stripboard, not a 100 mm square. The layer footprint is about 93 × 87 mm once the pillars in front and the rails and tie bars behind are counted, which leaves nothing usable on a square board. On a Eurocard the cube takes one end and the Nano, SD module, socket and capacitor live in the 67 × 100 mm left over.

Where to feed the supply

The order matters more than the position. Run the supply to the bulk capacitor first, then from the capacitor to the ladder and the ring — so the capacitor sits across the supply at the point the cube draws from, not off on a stub.

  • Socket to capacitor: 1.5 mm² wire, as short as the layout allows.
  • Capacitor to the VDD ladder: join at the middle strip, so current reaches the outer strips two at a time rather than travelling the length of the ladder.
  • Capacitor to the GND ring: one connection, near the same point.
  • The Nano's GND: to the GND ring close to where the data wire leaves for LED (0,0). The data signal returns through that connection; a short one keeps the loop small.
  • The Nano's 5 V: through the 1N5819 from the ladder, so the board runs on either USB or the brick without the two supplies meeting.
  • Per-layer decoupling: a 100 nF ceramic between any pillar and the nearest GND rail, one or two per layer at opposite corners. Cheap, and it stops the data line seeing the supply bounce when a bright frame lands.

No top-end injection needed. 0.9 mm copper is 2.2 mΩ per 100 mm. The worst-loaded conductor in the whole cube — a layer tie bar carrying 1.5 A over 81 mm — drops under 3 mV, and a pillar drops a fraction of a millivolt. The voltage drop people warn about in LED cubes is a problem of thin PCB traces and long thin leads, neither of which this design has. The two places that do matter are the stripboard strips, fixed by the reinforcing wire, and the DC connector.

Current and the connector

125 LEDs at full white is 7.5 A, which nobody runs. The sketch caps it:

#define MAX_MILLIAMPS 3000
FastLED.setMaxPowerInVoltsAndMilliamps(5, MAX_MILLIAMPS);

3 A across 125 LEDs is 24 mA each — about 40 % of full white with every LED lit, which on a diffused cube is already bright enough to be uncomfortable to look at directly. It also keeps a 5.5 × 2.5 mm chassis socket inside its rating, which the 5 A the supply could deliver would not. Raise the number later if the cube turns out dimmer than expected; it is one recompile.

Build order

  1. Confirm the pinout on one LED. 100 Ω in series with the 5 V feed. Right way round, the chip idles at about 1 mA and you measure 0.1 V across the resistor; wrong way round, about 4 V and it stays dark. Nothing can be destroyed either way.
  2. Test every LED before bending it. A 4-way socket on a breadboard and a colour cycle. Two seconds each, and it is the only cheap moment to find a dud — once one is soldered mid-chain, everything after it goes dark and you are cutting it out.
  3. Make the jig and bend all the legs: DIN left, DOUT right, GND back, VDD front, as drawn above. Bend 25 and build a trial layer before committing to the other 100.
  4. Build layer 0 flat on the jig. Rows alternate direction — rows 0, 2 and 4 with DIN to the left, rows 1 and 3 turned 180°. Tack the GND rails first, then the tie bars, then the data legs. Leave the VDD legs standing free; they attach to the pillars later.
  5. Test the layer before building the next. Feed it from the Nano with the 100 Ω in place and run chain_test: one LED at a time, in chain order. If the order is wrong you will see exactly where. Repeat for all five layers.
  6. Prepare the base board. Cut the strips between the cube area and the free area, solder 0.9 mm wire along each of the five VDD strips, add the spines at both ends and the GND ring. Fit the socket, capacitor, diode and Nano.
  7. Stand the 25 pillars in the base board and solder them to the strips. Check they are vertical and parallel in both axes before going further — everything stacks on them.
  8. Thread the layers on from the top down, spacing them 20.32 mm with offcuts of stripboard as gauges. Solder each layer's VDD legs to the pillars, then its tie bars to the corner risers. Test after every layer.
  9. Route the four data jumpers between layers, up the back-left corner alongside a GND riser.
  10. Add the decoupling caps, one or two per layer.
  11. First full power-up with the 100 Ω still in the supply line, then remove it and raise MAX_MILLIAMPS to taste.

Two habits worth keeping

100 Ω on every first power-up. New LED, new layer, new anything. It converts "that joint is wrong" from a bang into a reading on a meter.

Touch the LEDs after a few seconds. Dark but cool means a dry joint or two DIN pins tied together, which is harmless. Dark and warm means two DOUT pins tied together — two outputs fighting with nothing in series — and it will cook them both. Disconnect first and ask questions after.
